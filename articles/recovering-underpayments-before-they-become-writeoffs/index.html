Recovering Underpayments Before They Become Write-Offs

How payment integrity reviews catch silent revenue leakage—and a proof-of-concept approach to recover underpayments before they age into write-offs.

Denials get attention because they announce themselves. Underpayments often do not. When payers adjudicate below contracted rates and teams lack a variance routine, cash leaks quietly until balances age into write-offs.

Key Takeaways

  • Industry payment-integrity analyses consistently find material underpayment rates on commercial and government claims that cleared as “paid.”
  • Most recoverable leakage concentrates in fee-schedule variance, modifier mishandling, and bundled-payment misapplication—not exotic edge cases.
  • A 60–90 day underpayment pilot on one high-volume payer can prove recovery ROI before a full payment-integrity program is funded.

The Leakage Most Dashboards Miss

Revenue cycle scorecards usually track denial rate, days in A/R, and collection percentage. Those metrics matter—but they can look healthy while contracted underpayments accumulate. A claim marked paid is not necessarily paid correctly.

Silent revenue loss on “paid” claims

Payment integrity and contract-compliance reviews across multi-hospital systems routinely identify underpayment rates in the low-to-mid single digits on adjudicated claims—enough, at scale, to erase months of denial-recovery gains. HFMA and industry RCM analyses repeatedly flag fee-schedule mismatches and incorrect bundling as primary drivers.

Source: HFMA / industry payment-integrity and contract-variance analyses summarizing underpayment concentration on adjudicated commercial claims.

The operational problem is timing. Once balances age past timely-filing or contractual appeal windows, recovery options shrink. Waiting for month-end variance reports is often waiting too long.

Proof Of Concept: One Payer, One Service Line

You do not need a enterprise payment-integrity platform on day one. You need a controlled comparison between expected payment and remittance for a defined claim set.

  1. Baseline (2 weeks): Select one high-volume commercial payer and one specialty. Pull expected allowed amounts from the contract fee schedule and compare to remittance advice for the last 90 days.
  2. Triage (4–6 weeks): Flag variances above a dollar threshold. Categorize by fee-schedule mismatch, modifier/unit error, bundling, and secondary-payer sequencing.
  3. Recover and measure: Submit corrected claims or underpayment appeals on confirmed variances. Track recovered dollars, labor hours, and win rate by category.

Success is a positive net recovery after labor—and a clear map of which variance types recur. That map becomes the business case for automation and ongoing monitoring.

Controls That Keep Cash From Aging Away

Expected-vs-paid checks on every remittance batch. Treat contract variance as an operational exception, not a quarterly audit finding.

Owner and SLA for underpayment work queues. Assign recovery ownership the same way you assign denial ownership—with aging alerts before timely-filing cliffs.

Feed wins back to billing and coding. Recurring modifier or bundling variances are front-end defects. Fix the source, not only the remittance.
